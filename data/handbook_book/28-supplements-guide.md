## Chapter overview
Supplements come last in the order of importance, behind energy, protein, sticking with it, food quality and timing. For most people, the list worth buying is very short. It is not empty, though. Two have position stands behind them.

## Before buying anything
Ask yourself four questions. If any answer is no, your money is probably better spent elsewhere.
- Am I training consistently, and progressing on something?
- Am I reaching my protein target most days?
- Does my total food match my goal?
- Am I sleeping enough, and regularly?

## Creatine monohydrate
> evidence: Evidence: strong
> The ISSN calls creatine monohydrate "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available to athletes" for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training. {issncreatine}
> **Dose:** once muscles are saturated, 3 to 5 g a day generally maintains stores. Some studies suggest larger athletes may need 5 to 10 g a day. The fastest way to raise stores is 5 g four times a day for 5 to 7 days, but you do not have to do this. {issncreatine}
> **Safety:** the paper reports that short and long-term use, up to 30 g a day for 5 years, is safe and well tolerated in healthy people. It also reports that creatine either has no effect on or reduces injury, dehydration and cramping in athletes. {issncreatine}
What follows in practice:
- **Buy monohydrate.** It is the form the ISSN paper is about, and usually a low-cost one.
- **Take it daily at any time you will remember.** Stores build over time, so exact timing matters little.
- **Expect a small weight gain at first.** Creatine holds a little water. Your seven-day average may step up.
> warn: The safety statement has a limit
> That safety evidence is for healthy people. If you have kidney disease, any other diagnosed condition, or take prescription medicine, ask a clinician first. Levlofit does not advise on supplements alongside disease.

## Caffeine
> evidence: Evidence: strong for endurance, moderate for strength
> The ISSN says caffeine "has been shown to acutely enhance various aspects of exercise performance in many but not all studies". Aerobic endurance shows the most consistent moderate to large benefit. Benefits for strength and muscular endurance are small to moderate. {issncaffeine}
> **Dose:** 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight has consistently improved performance. The minimum may be as low as 2 mg per kg. Very high doses, such as 9 mg per kg, bring more side effects and do not seem to be needed. {issncaffeine}
> **Timing:** most commonly about 60 minutes before exercise. {issncaffeine}
> **Differences between people:** genes and your usual caffeine intake may change how you respond, including effects on sleep and anxiety. {issncaffeine}
> warn: Caffeine and sleep
> Sleep loss lowers performance. {craven2022} A dose before training that costs you sleep is a poor trade, so if you train in the evening, try less caffeine or none.
Coffee and tea contain caffeine, so you do not need a pre-workout product to get it. Blends that hide their doses cannot be judged.

## Protein powder
This is food in a convenient form. The ISSN calls it a practical way to get enough protein, while noting you can meet your needs from whole foods. {issnprotein} Buy it if it makes your target easier to reach, and compare its cost per gram of protein with food first.

## Vitamins and minerals: correct a deficiency, do not guess at one
These are general notes, not advice for you personally.
| Supplement | When it makes sense | Note |
| Vitamin B12 | Needed on a fully plant-based diet. | No expected benefit if your levels are already fine. |
| Vitamin D | When sun exposure is limited or your levels are low. | Correcting a deficiency makes sense. Taking excess does not. |
| Iron | Only for a confirmed deficiency. | **Do not self-prescribe.** Too much iron is harmful. Get a blood test and see a clinician. |
| Omega-3 | When you eat little long-chain omega-3, particularly on plant-based diets. | Not a performance supplement. |
| Multivitamin | As low-cost cover for a narrow diet. | It does not replace food. |
> warn: Blood results
> Levlofit does not interpret blood results. The right order is a test, then a clinician's reading, then a dose if you need one. This matters even more during pregnancy or breastfeeding, with any diagnosed condition, or with prescription medicine.

## Not worth your money
These have no strong case behind them in this Handbook's sources. That is not proof they can never work, but the burden of proof sits with the product.
| Product | Why not |
| Testosterone boosters | No strong reason to expect a meaningful change in your body composition. |
| Fat burners | Fat loss is driven by a calorie deficit. {aragon2017} If one contains caffeine, you are paying extra for caffeine. |
| Detox products | Your liver and kidneys already do this job. |
| BCAAs and EAAs, if your protein is enough | Protein already supplies them, and the ISSN suggests whole foods with all essential amino acids. {issnprotein} |
| Glutamine for muscle growth | No strong reason to expect a benefit if your protein is adequate. |
| Proprietary blends | If a blend hides each ingredient's dose, it cannot be checked against evidence. |

## Quality and contamination
> warn: The label is not a guarantee
> Supplements are checked less strictly than medicines. Labels may be inaccurate, and contamination or undeclared ingredients can happen. This Handbook gives no figure for how common this is.
> Prefer products with independent third-party testing, where available.
> Prefer single ingredients with clear doses over blends.
> Be sceptical of promises of fast or dramatic results, and of unofficial sellers.
> If you are a tested athlete, use only certified products.

## Myths
| Claim | Standing |
| "Supplements build muscle." | Misleading. Training supplies the signal, and food and sleep support it. Creatine helps the training you can do. It does not replace it. |
| "Creatine damages the kidneys." | Not supported in healthy people. Kidney disease is a separate matter for your doctor. {issncreatine} |
| "Creatine causes cramps and dehydration." | Not supported. Athlete studies reported no effect or less of both. {issncreatine} |
| "You must load creatine." | False. Loading is faster. A daily dose gets there more slowly. {issncreatine} |
| "Protein powder is a steroid." | False. It is concentrated food protein. |
| "Natural means safe." | False. Plant extracts can be active and can interact with medicines. |

## Key takeaways
- Energy, protein, sticking with it and sleep come before any supplement.
- Creatine monohydrate is the one most worth considering: 3 to 5 g a day, with a strong safety record in healthy people.
- Caffeine works best for endurance, and less consistently for strength. Do not trade sleep for it.
- Protein powder is convenient food. Compare it with real food on price.
- Correct a confirmed deficiency. Do not guess at one.
- Blends that hide their doses cannot be judged.
