## What a leg session trains
A leg session trains your quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes and calves. These include some of your largest muscles, and their big lifts are among the hardest most people do, so order matters a lot.

## Slots and coverage
Go from the heaviest, most technical lift down to single-joint work that handles fatigue well. Include a squat pattern and a hip hinge, because they load the front and back of your leg in different ways.
> evidence: Evidence: emerging
> Thirty trained people did different exercises with each leg, twice a week for 8 weeks. Leg extension favoured the rectus femoris, part of the quadriceps. Leg press favoured the vastus lateralis, another part. Straight-leg calf raises favoured one calf muscle, the medial gastrocnemius, with no clear result for the lateral gastrocnemius or soleus. This is a single study. {burke2025}
So big lifts may not make isolation work unnecessary, and the reverse may be true too.
| Slot | Job |
| Main squat pattern | The heaviest, most technical lift, done first. |
| Hip hinge | Hamstrings and glutes, which the squat pattern does not fully cover. |
| Second quad lift | Usually a machine, adding quad work at lower technical cost. |
| Hamstring isolation | Direct knee-bending work. |
| Quad isolation | Direct knee-straightening work. |
| Calves | A straight-leg raise, a bent-knee raise, or both if you have the equipment. |
Filling two slots with squat patterns and skipping the hinge trains the front of your leg twice and neglects the back.

## Choosing an exercise
The four tests from [[push-sessions|Building a push session]] apply. Joints matter especially here: a barbell squat loads your spine in a way a leg press or hack squat does not, so a lower-back problem can be reason enough to choose the supported option.

## Swapping exercises
A barbell back squat and a hack squat can replace each other. Both are squat patterns using your knees and hips. A leg press cannot replace a Romanian deadlift: one is a squat pattern and the other a hinge. Swapping them leaves the hinge slot empty.

## Range of motion and partial repetitions
Full range is the default. The ACSM found strength improved with a complete range of motion, though it found not enough data on range and growth. Load gained by shortening your range is not progress. {acsm2026}
"Partial repetitions are always wrong" goes too far, though.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate for long over short; insufficient for partials over full range
> In a review of 8 studies, training at longer muscle lengths, the stretched part of a movement, gave more growth than training at shorter lengths (effect size 0.283, 0.04 to 0.52). The advantage was clear at the far and central parts of the muscle. {strey2026}
> The review compared these two partial ranges with each other. It does not show that partials beat a full range.
In practice:
- Train through a full range by default.
- A partial done on purpose in the stretched position is a fair tool, such as when a joint limits the other part of the range.
- A partial done because the load is too heavy is still a mistake. Decide before the set, not after.
- A quarter squat cuts out the stretched part, which is the part this evidence favours.

## Volume, effort and frequency
The evidence in [[push-sessions|Building a push session]] applies. No fixed recovery time for legs, such as 72 hours, is established.
In practice, heavy squats and hinges are demanding, and many people handle them less often than pressing or pulling. Test that against your own log. The claim that calves recover fast enough to train daily is **not established**. Train them as often as you can sustain and progress.

## How to progress
The three models from the push chapter apply. Adjusting by feel can suit squats and hinges, since it may catch a tired day before your technique breaks down.
For strength, the ACSM links loads of 80% of your one-repetition maximum or more with greater gains. For growth, load from 30% to 100% made no difference, which is how a leg extension can build quadriceps despite a modest number on the stack. {acsm2026}

## Warm-up
Use the same three parts, aimed at your hips and ankles. Warm-up sets matter most here, where your main lift is heaviest. Tight ankles can limit squat depth. It is **not established** that warming them up changes your results, so test whether your own depth improves, and keep the work if it does.

## Across the week
A leg session's demand often makes it the session to plan around. Remember that a heavy hinge shares work with pulling sessions through your lower back and grip.

## Worked example
This is one example, not a prescription.
| Slot | Exercise | Sets | Repetitions | Rest |
| Main squat | Barbell back squat | 4 | 6 to 8 | 2 to 3 min |
| Hip hinge | Romanian deadlift | 3 | 8 to 10 | 2 to 3 min |
| Second quad lift | Leg press | 3 | 10 to 12 | 90 sec |
| Hamstring isolation | Seated leg curl | 3 | 10 to 12 | 75 to 90 sec |
| Quad isolation | Leg extension | 3 | 12 to 15 | 60 to 90 sec |
| Calves | Standing calf raise | 4 | 12 to 15 | 60 to 90 sec |
Leg press and leg extension are both here on purpose, because they favoured different parts of the quadriceps. {burke2025} The seated curl keeps your hip bent, which holds your hamstrings at a longer length.

## Common mistakes
- **Cutting depth to add load.** A quarter squat is a different, easier exercise.
- **Skipping the hinge.** This is a common gap in leg sessions.
- **Assuming big lifts make isolation useless.** Early evidence points the other way.
- **Bouncing out of the bottom of a squat.** Stay in control where the load on your muscles is greatest.
- **Scheduling by a fixed clock.** Use your log.
- **Skipping calves because progress is slow.** Slow is not the same as absent.

## Key takeaways
- Cover both squat and hip-hinge patterns.
- Big lifts and isolation work may develop different parts of a muscle.
- Put your main lift first.
- Full range is the default. A deliberate partial in the stretched position is defensible. One caused by too much load is not.
- No fixed recovery time is established for legs. Check your own log.
- Load drives strength. For size, hard sets matter more than the number on the bar.
