summary: A real effect, much smaller than advertised.

## Chapter overview
Meal timing is oversold, though it is not nothing either. It helps in some situations. Those situations are narrower than the marketing claims, and wider than "it does not matter".

## Where timing sits
| What | Effect | Grade |
| Total daily energy | Decides whether weight goes up or down | Strong {aragon2017} |
| Total daily protein | Helps decide how much of the change is muscle | Strong {issnprotein} |
| Spreading protein across the day | Real, but secondary. Every 3 to 4 hours is the stated ideal | Moderate {issnprotein} |
| Eating around training | Can support performance and recovery | Moderate {kerksick2017} |
| Carbohydrate timing | Matters when you need rapid recovery. Otherwise much less important | Moderate {kerksick2017} |
| Number of meals | Limited effect on weight loss and body composition when not exercising; can help appetite | Moderate {kerksick2017} |
| Timing to the minute | None shown | Insufficient |

## The anabolic window
The claim is that you must have protein within about 30 minutes of training or the session is wasted. The position stands do not support that.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate
> The ISSN protein paper says the best time is likely a matter of what you tolerate, since benefits come from eating before or after training. The effect of exercise lasts at least 24 hours, though it likely fades with time. {issnprotein}
> The ISSN timing paper says protein within two hours after exercise strongly stimulates muscle protein synthesis, and that the size and timing of your meal before training affects how much post-training protein matters. {kerksick2017}
So think in hours, not minutes, and remember the meal before training counts as well.

## Before training
The goal is to feel fuelled and comfortable. Two patterns work:
- **A normal meal a few hours before.** Protein and carbohydrate, with moderate fat and fibre so you are comfortable when you start.
- **A lighter snack closer to training.** Mostly carbohydrate, with some protein, and low in fat and fibre.
Indian options include curd with fruit, poha or upma, oats with milk, a banana with milk, rice with dal, toast with peanut butter, and roti with paneer.
No exact timing is established. What feels comfortable varies with the person and the meal, so try it and see.
### Training early, or without eating
Training without food is a scheduling choice. In practice it suits some people and not others. If eating first makes you uncomfortable, train and eat afterwards. If you feel weak or light-headed, eat something small first. If that continues, or if you have diabetes or another medical condition, ask your doctor.

## After training
Have a meal with protein and carbohydrate within the next few hours. That is the whole recommendation.
You do not need a shake before leaving the gym. It is also fine if that is the easiest way to reach your daily protein.
> warn: When carbohydrate timing really matters
> Fast glycogen refuelling plans are for when you need rapid recovery, such as training twice in one day or competing on back-to-back days. {kerksick2017}
> The ISSN paper says that outside such situations, when daily carbohydrate matches your needs, the importance of timed carbohydrate is "notably decreased". {kerksick2017}
> If you train once a day, eat enough carbohydrate across the day and leave it there.

## How many meals
The number of meals is largely a practical choice. Changing meal frequency has shown limited effect on weight loss and body composition when not exercising, with stronger evidence for appetite and fullness. {kerksick2017}
| Pattern | Suits |
| Three main meals | Most people. Fits typical Indian eating. |
| Three meals plus one or two protein snacks | Anyone struggling to reach a protein target. |
| Fewer, larger meals | People who like big meals, or whose schedule prevents regular eating. Protein takes more planning. |
| More, smaller meals | People who need a lot of food and find big meals uncomfortable. |
The ISSN suggests spreading protein every 3 to 4 hours, which suits three meals plus a snack. {issnprotein} If your plan includes pre- and post-workout meals, they count towards this.

## What usually goes wrong
- **Skipping meals to save calories, then overeating later.** This is really a total-food problem.
- **Training after a long gap without food, then blaming the plan.** Food belongs on your stall checklist.
- **Eating a large, fatty, high-fibre meal right before training.** It can be uncomfortable.
- **Trying new food on a day that matters.** Test it on an ordinary day first.
- **Perfecting your timing while your daily total goes unmeasured.** Fix the big things first.
- **Treating a post-workout shake as a requirement.** It is a convenience.

## Key takeaways
- The effect of training lasts at least a day. Think in hours, not minutes.
- Total energy and protein come first. Timing is a refinement.
- Spreading protein every 3 to 4 hours is the stated ideal.
- Fast carbohydrate plans are for rapid recovery, not ordinary training.
- Meal frequency is a practical choice.
- Training without eating first is a scheduling choice.
