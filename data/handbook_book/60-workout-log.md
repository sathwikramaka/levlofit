## Chapter overview
This Handbook keeps telling you to read your log. The reason is about measurement, not motivation. Other tools give an estimate. Your log gives a **count**: repetitions done, at a load you can read off the bar.

## Why the log beats the other tools
| Tool | Error |
| Your log | Almost none in the recording. Its ups and downs are real changes in performance. |
| Body weight | Small tool error, large daily changes. Averaging handles these. |
| Tape | Technique error. |
| Photos | Lighting and pose. |
| Body-fat scales | Poor at tracking change. {merrigan2022} |
It also records your training directly. Weekly sets help drive growth, and your log is a direct record of them. {pelland2025}

## What to record
| Field | Why |
| Exercise | Comparisons only work within the same movement. |
| Load | Counted, not estimated. |
| Repetitions, per set | What you did, not what you planned. |
| Working sets | These add up to your weekly volume. |
| How close to failure, if you use it | Optional. This one is estimated. |
| A short note | Sleep, illness or a rushed session can explain a bad day. |
Levlofit logs your sets as you train. Look at last session's numbers before each exercise, so they can inform the decision you are about to make.

## An example log
These numbers show the format, not a recommendation.
| Exercise | Load | Reps per set | Note |
| Barbell bench press | 60 kg | 8, 8, 7 | Slept badly |
| Lat pulldown | 50 kg | 10, 10, 9 | None |
A row saying "3 x 8" would hide that the last set fell short. That shortfall and the note are the useful signal.

## What counts as progress
| Route | Status |
| More load at the same reps | Progress. |
| More reps at the same load | Progress. |
| More weekly sets for the muscle | Progress, with a clear link to growth and a real recovery cost. {pelland2025} |
| Shorter rest | Not progress. It is a time choice. |
| Better technique | Worth having, though your log cannot count it. |

## Signal and noise in your log
These thresholds are practical judgement, not research findings.
| What you see | Reading |
| One set short of last week | Noise. |
| One whole session worse | Usually noise. Read your note. |
| Two or three sessions of a lift flat or falling | Worth looking into. See [[plateaus|Breaking through plateaus]]. |
| Several lifts falling together | Look at sleep, food, stress or a deficit that is too hard. |
| Log rising while scale and tape are flat | Your log is more sensitive. Wait. |

## How close to failure, if you log it
This is the one field here that is estimated. The main analysis of it is exploratory, and its RIR values were estimated rather than measured. {robinson2024} Log it if it helps you manage effort. Do not treat it as equal to load and repetitions.

## When your log slows down
Progress slows for everyone. Before changing your plan, check:
- Sleep. {craven2022}
- Food and protein.
- Life stress, which mostly costs sessions. {stults2014}
- Whether your volume is recoverable at all.

## A caution about motivation
A log that shows you getting better at something can support your sense of competence, which is linked with continuing to exercise. {teixeira2012} A log that becomes a standard you must meet every session is different. If missing a target brings distress far beyond what one session costs, see the warnings in [[aesthetic-physique|Building an aesthetic physique]].

## Common mistakes
- **Recording your plan, not what happened.**
- **Recording a set total, not reps per set.**
- **Treating shorter rest as overload.**
- **Reading one bad session as a plateau.**
- **Changing your plan before checking sleep, food, stress and volume.**
- **Treating an estimated closeness to failure as a count.**
- **Comparing your loads with someone else's.**

## Key takeaways
- Your log is a count, not an estimate.
- It records your training directly.
- Record load, reps per set, sets and a note.
- Shorter rest is not overload.
- One bad session is noise. Two or three flat sessions are a question.
- When your log stalls, look at sleep, food, stress and volume first.
