## Chapter overview
[[recovery|Recovery]] shows what sleep loss costs you. This chapter looks the other way: how much sleep is recommended, what happens when you sleep more on purpose, and which familiar sleep advice has evidence behind it.

## How much sleep
> evidence: Evidence: strong, by consensus
> A joint statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society supports 7 or more hours a night, regularly, for adults aged 18 to 60. {aasm2015}
Notice that this is a floor, not a range. Seven hours is the minimum to aim for. If you often wake unrested after seven, try more.

## Sleeping more on purpose
> evidence: Evidence: moderate, from limited studies
> A review of 25 sleep intervention studies in athletes found that sleeping longer at night and napping were the most effective of the methods tested for improving performance. The authors acknowledge the limited amount of high-quality evidence. {cunha2023}
You cannot decide to fall asleep. You can decide to be in bed earlier. If you are often short of sleep, this is likely to be the most useful change in this chapter.

## Sleep hygiene: a surprising result
> warn: The standard advice has thin support in athletes
> The same review found sleep hygiene advice and removing electronic devices at night had no effect on sleep and later performance. These results come from only a few studies. {cunha2023}
So:
- **Do not stop these habits if they help you.** They are free, and a few studies do not prove they never work.
- **Do not expect much from them alone.** If your sleep is truly poor, a neater bedtime routine may not be the fix.
- **Put your effort into time in bed.** That has better support.
| Habit | Standing |
| Enough time in bed | Has the better support. {cunha2023} |
| Steady wake time, including weekends | Common advice, low cost. |
| Dark, cool, quiet room | Common advice, low cost. |
| Limiting late caffeine | Sensible. Caffeine can affect sleep, and this varies between people. {issncaffeine} |
| Fewer screens before bed | Removing devices showed no effect in a few athlete studies. Keep it if it helps you. {cunha2023} |
| Alcohol before bed | It can make you drowsy and still disturb your sleep. |

## Naps
The same review found naps among the most effective methods overall. {cunha2023} A nap is a reasonable option if you are short of sleep. It does not replace your night's sleep. Keep it early enough that it does not push back your bedtime, and if you nap before a session, notice whether you actually perform better.

## Sleep and injury
You will often hear that poor sleep causes injury. A review of 12 cohort studies concluded that current evidence does not support poor sleep as an independent risk factor for injury in adult athletes, and that the studies were too mixed to settle the question. {dobrosielski2021}
The case for sleep rests on performance, and that is strong enough.

## What sleep loss costs
In a large review, sleep loss lowered exercise performance by about 7.6% on average. Afternoon and evening sessions consistently suffered, while morning sessions were largely unaffected. If a bad night is unavoidable and your session cannot move, train earlier if you can. {craven2022}

## Catching up
The consensus recommendation is for regular sleep. {aasm2015} It is **not established** that weekend catch-up fully reverses a week of short nights. Treat extra sleep as useful and partial: better than nothing, and worse than not building the debt.

## When poor sleep is a medical problem
> warn: See a clinician
> If your sleep stays bad, more routine tips may not be the answer, and looking for them may delay real help. See a qualified clinician if you have:
> Trouble falling or staying asleep over several weeks, despite enough time in bed.
> Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, gasping or choking in your sleep, or waking unrefreshed after long sleep.
> Overwhelming daytime sleepiness, or falling asleep without meaning to.
> Sleep problems alongside lasting low mood, anxiety or loss of interest.
> Restless, uncomfortable feelings in your legs at night.
> Insomnia and other sleep disorders are medical conditions with treatments. Levlofit does not diagnose them or recommend sleep medicine.

## Common mistakes
- **Treating seven hours as the whole target.** The statement says 7 or more.
- **Perfecting the routine instead of the duration.** Time in bed has better support.
- **Expecting routine changes to fix real insomnia.** If they have not worked in a few weeks, see a clinician.
- **Napping into the evening.** It can eat into your night's sleep.
- **Using injury claims as motivation.** The performance case is real without them.

## Key takeaways
- Aim for 7 or more hours a night, regularly.
- Spending longer in bed is the change with the better support.
- Naps can help. They do not replace your night's sleep.
- Sleep hygiene and removing devices showed no effect in a few athlete studies.
- It is not established that poor sleep alone causes injury.
- Sleep problems that last deserve a clinician.
