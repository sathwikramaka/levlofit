## Chapter overview
Recovery tools are sold on a promise: use them and you will adapt faster. Several have evidence for making you less sore, which is a smaller claim. And one popular tool has evidence of harming the strength gains you train for, so it comes first.

## Cold-water immersion: be careful
> evidence: Evidence: moderate, and negative for strength
> A review of eight articles on regular cold-water immersion, at 15 °C or below, alongside training found a harmful effect on one-repetition maximum, maximum isometric strength and strength endurance (effect size -0.60, -0.87 to -0.33). It did not appear to affect aerobic performance. {malta2021}
> warn: What this means for you
> If you lift to get stronger, do not make cold-water immersion after sessions a routine habit. You may be trading less soreness for smaller strength gains.
> It may be a fair trade when you must perform again soon, such as during a tournament. Treat that as a deliberate choice, not a default.
A quick cold shower because you like it is not what was studied.

## The question to ask
| Question | What the evidence says |
| Does it reduce soreness? | For several tools, yes, from a large but weak literature. {wiecha2025} |
| Does it speed up performance recovery? | Not established here for these tools. |
| Does it improve your training results? | No tool here has shown this. Regular cold-water immersion had a harmful effect on strength. {malta2021} |

## Deciding whether a tool is worth it
- **Are sleep, food and training volume handled?** If not, no tool will make up for them.
- **What are you buying?** Usually feeling better. That is fine, as long as you know it.
- **What does it cost?** Money, time and, for regular cold immersion, possibly strength gains.
> tip: In practice
> If a tool makes you feel better and you enjoy it, use it. That can help you keep turning up. Just do not spend money expecting results no evidence has promised.

## What you do not need
- **Anything promising faster recovery.** No tool here has shown it.
- **A long routine after every session.** You will likely drop it.
- **A wearable recovery score that overrules your log.** Treat it as a weak input.
- **Supplements sold for recovery.** They do not replace sleep or food.

## Safety notes
> warn: Use these tools sensibly
> **Cold immersion** carries risks from sudden cold and from staying in too long. If you have a heart condition, ask a clinician first. Do not immerse alone in open water.
> **Saunas and hot baths** carry risks with dehydration, alcohol, pregnancy and some medical conditions.
> **Massage guns and deep pressure** should not be used over an injury, joint, bone or the neck, or over an area that is numb, swollen or painful.
> **Compression** is not right for everyone, especially with circulation problems or a history of blood clots.
> If you have a diagnosed condition, ask a clinician before trying any of these.

## Common mistakes
- **Routine cold plunges after lifting.** See above.
- **Buying tools before fixing sleep and food.**
- **Reading "less soreness" as "faster recovery".** They are different claims.
- **Ranking every recovery method on one scale.** Sleep and a massage gun are not the same kind of thing.

## Key takeaways
- Sleep, food and sensible training volume come first. The rest is optional.
- Several tools reduce soreness, on weak evidence.
- Regular cold-water immersion harmed strength gains. Do not make it a routine if you lift for strength.
- Foam rolling and massage guns are fine for comfort.
- Feeling better is a fair reason to use something. Just call it that.
