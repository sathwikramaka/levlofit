## Chapter overview
A small budget changes which foods you use, not what your body needs. Total energy, protein and sticking with it still decide your result.
> tip: Practical guidance
> Prices and availability vary by city, season and household, so most of this chapter is practical advice rather than research. The protein figures come from food composition tables.

## The number to compare
When money is tight, compare **cost per gram of protein**, not price per kilogram. Protein is usually the expensive part of your food, and carbohydrate is usually cheap.
> tip: Work it out once
> Divide the pack price by the grams of protein in the pack. Do this for the five or six sources you can actually buy. You only need to do it occasionally, and the cheapest food by weight is often not the cheapest protein.
Dry soya chunks and pulses often work out cheap per gram of protein, dairy often sits in the middle, and paneer, whey and meat are often more expensive. Check your own local prices, since this is a starting guess.

These are for planning. Where a pack has a label, trust the label. Curd is useful too, and it can cost less set at home from milk. Check the label on packaged curd for its protein.

## Everything else is usually cheap
Rice, atta, oats, potatoes, bananas, and seasonal fruit and vegetables are usually inexpensive. For fat, peanuts, seeds and ordinary cooking oil do the job. If you have money left over, a wider range of vegetables and enough fruit will make your food more pleasant, and healthier.

## A low-cost day
| Meal | Example | What it adds |
| Breakfast | Oats cooked in milk, with a banana | Protein from milk, carbohydrate, little skill needed |
| Lunch | Rice, dal, a vegetable, curd | A standard meal, often close already. A larger serving of dal is an easy upgrade. |
| Snack | Peanuts, curd or an egg | Cheap protein when you might otherwise skip it |
| Dinner | Soya chunk curry with rice or roti, plus a vegetable | A big, low-cost protein serving |
Notice this is not a special diet. It is an ordinary Indian day with the protein parts made bigger. That is often the cheapest way to your target, and the easiest to keep up.

## If you cannot cook
Hostel, shared kitchen, no time or no skill? These still work:
- Milk and curd, with no preparation.
- Sattu mixed into water or milk.
- Roasted chana, which keeps well and travels easily.
- Peanuts.
- Boiled eggs, if you can boil water.
- Oats soaked overnight in milk with fruit.
- Whey, if its cost per gram of protein works for you.
If your meals come from a mess or canteen, add to what is served: extra dal or curd at the meal, and a protein snack in your room. You can tell Levlofit about mess meals in your diet settings.

## Saving money without losing your target
- **Buy dry staples in bulk** if you can store them. Rice, dal, soya chunks and oats keep well.
- **Buy seasonal produce.** It is usually cheaper.
- **Cook once, eat several times.** Batch cooking dal or chana saves fuel and time.
- **Carry water.** Sugary drinks cost money and add calories without much fullness.
- **Make curd or paneer at home** if you can.
- **Compare supplements with food before you buy.**
> warn: Batch cooking and food safety
> Batch cooking is a main budget strategy, but it can be risky if handled carelessly, particularly with cooked rice and pulses in hot weather.
> Cool cooked food quickly, refrigerate it promptly and reheat it thoroughly. Do not keep leftovers at room temperature for long. If your fridge is unreliable, cook smaller amounts more often.

## Common mistakes
- **Assuming eating well must be expensive.** Soya chunks, dal, eggs and milk can be affordable.
- **Buying supplements before food.** Food often gives you more protein for the money.
- **Skipping meals to save money.** It cuts energy, and can lead to overeating later.
- **Paying for "fitness" branded food.** Compare its protein and price with ordinary food.
- **Forgetting to count dal.** Protein already in your meals counts.
- **Cutting protein first.** It is the part most likely to go when money is short, and one that matters most.

## Key takeaways
- Your targets do not change with your budget. Your foods do.
- Compare cost per gram of protein.
- Soya chunks, dal, chana, milk, curd and eggs can carry most of the load.
- The cheapest plan is usually an ordinary Indian day with bigger protein servings.
- No-cook options work: sattu, curd, roasted chana, peanuts and soaked oats.
- Take care with cooked rice and pulses in warm weather.
