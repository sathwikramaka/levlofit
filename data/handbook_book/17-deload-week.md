## Chapter overview
A deload is a planned or chosen period of less training stress. The idea is near-universal among coaches: build up fatigue, pull back, return fresher. It is also under-researched, and this chapter keeps what is reasoned, what coaches agree on and what has been measured apart.
> evidence: Evidence: insufficient that deloading is required
> An international panel of strength and physique coaches calls deloading "ubiquitous yet under-researched", and says how to fit it into training for the best results is unknown. The authors also say it is unclear whether deloading is a necessary part of training to avoid the harmful effects of overtraining. What follows is expert opinion and practical experience, not trial results. {bell2023}

## What a deload is
The panel's definition: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training." {bell2023}
So you keep training, with less stress. Its purpose is to prepare you for what comes next. It does not build anything by itself. It is also different from a **taper**, which peaks you for a competition.

## Why deload, claim by claim
| Claim | Grade | Comment |
| Reduces fatigue | Practical | It follows from doing less. |
| Improves later performance | Insufficient | Coaches widely report it. Controlled trials are lacking. {bell2023} |
| Protects joints and tendons | Insufficient | Plausible, not established. |
| Reduces mental fatigue | Practical | A real, fair reason. |
| Needed to avoid overtraining | Insufficient | The authors leave this open. {bell2023} |
That makes deloading **low cost and low certainty**. It is reasonable to adopt, as long as nobody claims more for it.

## When to deload
The paper's background reports that deloads are generally done every 4 to 6 weeks for about 7 days, ranging from one session to two weeks. That describes common practice, not a trial result. {bell2023} So this Handbook sets no required schedule.
Coaches on the panel agreed a deload could be planned in advance or decided by how you feel. {bell2023} The default here is the second: reduce when your own signals say so. Watch for a pattern:
- Performance falling across several unrelated exercises for more than a week.
- Normal warm-up loads feeling heavy.
- Joint aches that outlast sessions and build week to week.
- Trouble falling asleep, or waking early despite being tired.
- Appetite and motivation both low for several days with no other reason.
One of these is noise. Several together, lasting, are a reason to reduce. If you prefer a planned lighter week, that is also fine.

## How to reduce
The clearest point in that paper is universal agreement that **training volume should come down**, through fewer sets or repetitions. On intensity, the coaches accepted more than one approach: it could stay the same or drop. {bell2023}
So reduce volume first. Lowering the weight while keeping every set is unlikely to be the best version.
| What | Normal week | Lighter week |
| Hard sets | Full weekly volume | Clearly fewer. This is the main lever. |
| Load | Working loads | Same or lower. Pick one, so you can read the next week. |
| Effort | Final repetitions hard | Well short of hard. |
| Exercises | Normal | Unchanged. A lighter week is a poor time for new movements. |
| Schedule | Normal | Usually unchanged, which keeps the habit. Fewer days also works. |
| Food and sleep | Normal | Normal. Eating less works against the purpose. |
Here is an example of a pressing session:
| Exercise | Normal week | Lighter week |
| Barbell bench press | 4 sets, last repetitions hard, 80 kg | 2 sets, well short of hard, 65 kg |
| Incline machine press | 3 sets, last repetitions hard | 2 sets, comfortable |
| Cable fly | 3 sets | 1 set, or skip |
| Lateral raise | 3 sets | 2 sets, comfortable |
| Triceps pushdown | 3 sets | 1 set, or skip |
Sixteen hard sets become about eight comfortable ones. Nothing should feel difficult. If the week leaves you as tired as normal, you did not reduce enough.

## Reduce or stop?
A full break is right when you are ill, injured, or dealing with something that needs the time. [[recovery|Recovery]] lists the symptoms that need a clinician instead of a training change.
Otherwise, reducing is usually the better choice, for a practical reason: the session you still turn up for is easier to continue from than the one you skipped.

## Common mistakes
- **Turning a lighter week into a normal one.** If every set still ends hard, volume came down only on paper.
- **Lowering weight but keeping every set.** Reduce sets first.
- **Testing yourself.** A personal record attempt defeats the purpose.
- **Eating less.** Recovery has a cost.
- **Using it to rescue a plan you cannot recover from.** If you need a lighter week very often, your usual volume may be too high.

## Key takeaways
- A deload means less training stress, not stopping.
- It is under-researched, and whether it is necessary is unresolved.
- No required schedule is established. Let your signals guide you, or plan one if you prefer.
- Coaches agree that volume should come down. They accept different approaches to intensity.
- It is low cost and low certainty. Reasonable to adopt, unreasonable to insist on.
