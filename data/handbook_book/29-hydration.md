title: Hydration and electrolytes
summary: One number worth knowing, and two ways to get it wrong.

## Chapter overview
One common claim says drink constantly and hit a fixed daily target. Another says thirst handles everything. The evidence is more contextual. Past a certain point, losing fluid impairs performance. Thirst is a good guide in some conditions and a poor one in others. And drinking too much can be dangerous.

## The number that matters
> evidence: Evidence: moderate
> Dehydration of 2% of body mass or more is associated with poorer temperature control, more strain on the heart and, in many conditions, poorer aerobic performance. These conditions include warmer, longer and more intense exercise. In 34 endurance studies with 60 observations, 68% showed significantly impaired performance at this level. {kenefick2018}
> Many of these studies were done in a laboratory, which may not match real conditions. {kenefick2018}
Two per cent of body mass is 1.4 kg for a 70 kg person. Body mass losses this large take time to build up. {kenefick2018} This figure concerns aerobic work. It is **not established** that the same threshold applies to resistance training.

## When to trust thirst
Thirst is a real signal, with a known limit: it tends to ease before you are fully rehydrated. In one study people drinking freely replaced about half of what they lost. When you sweat a lot, that deficit can build up. {kenefick2018}
| Situation | Strategy |
| Exercise under about 60 to 90 minutes, in cooler conditions or at lower intensity | Drinking to thirst may be enough. {kenefick2018} |
| Exercise over 90 minutes, especially in heat | Planned drinking is better. {kenefick2018} |
| Heavy sweating, or sessions where performance matters | Planned drinking. Find your sweat rate. {kenefick2018} |
| Indian summer heat, or a gym without air conditioning | In practice, treat this like the hot case, however short the session. |

## Drinking too much
> warn: Exercise-associated hyponatremia
> Drinking far more than your body can get rid of dilutes the sodium in your blood. This is mainly a risk in long endurance events rather than gym training, and it can be life-threatening.
> Its symptoms can look like dehydration: headache, nausea, confusion and dizziness. Swollen hands or face are also warning signs. Drinking more can make it worse.
> **A clear rule: never drink so much that you gain weight during exercise.** {kenefick2018}
> Suspected hyponatremia is a medical emergency. Get urgent medical care.

## Measure your sweat rate
If you train long, hard or in heat, one session can replace a lot of guessing. This is a practical method, and it estimates fluid lost. It does not precisely measure how hydrated you are.
- Weigh yourself just before training, in minimal clothing.
- Note everything you drink during the session.
- Weigh yourself again right after, towelled dry, in the same clothing.
- Weight lost plus fluid drunk is roughly your fluid loss. One kilogram is about one litre. {kenefick2018}
Try it once in cool conditions and once in heat to learn your own range.

## Daily intake
Needs vary with your body size, climate, activity and the water in your food, so no single number fits everyone. Levlofit shows a daily water goal, and you can set your own. Treat it as a reminder, not a rule. In practice:
- Drink across the day. Tea, coffee, milk, dal and curd count too.
- Drink more in heat and around longer or harder sessions.
- Start sessions hydrated.
- Replace fluid over the hours after a session, not all at once.
**Urine colour** is a rough check. Pale yellow suggests you are drinking enough, and consistently dark suggests otherwise. Some vitamins and medicines can change it.

## Electrolytes
Electrolytes are minerals in your body fluids, involved in fluid balance and in nerve and muscle function.
| Electrolyte | Why it matters for training |
| Sodium | The main one lost in sweat, and the one involved in hyponatremia. |
| Potassium | Lost in smaller amounts. Fruit, vegetables, dal and milk supply it. |
| Magnesium, calcium | Lost in small amounts. Important in your diet, but rarely a hydration concern. |
> warn: Cramp
> Cramp is often blamed on electrolyte loss or dehydration. A competing view blames nerve and muscle fatigue. This is **contested**, so do not assume electrolytes will prevent it. Frequent or severe cramps, or cramps at rest, need a clinician.

## Do you need a sports drink?
These are practical suggestions.
| Situation | What is enough |
| Ordinary gym session in normal conditions | Water. |
| Long session, heavy sweating or real heat | Fluid with some salt. Nimbu paani with salt, or an ORS-style drink, can do this cheaply. |
| Endurance exercise over about 90 minutes | Fluid, sodium and carbohydrate. |
| Trying to lose fat | Remember that sports drinks contain calories. |
If you have high blood pressure, or kidney or heart disease, ask your doctor about added salt.

## Common mistakes
- **Treating a daily target as a requirement.** No one number fits everyone.
- **Assuming thirst is always enough.** It becomes less reliable as time, heat and sweat rise.
- **Assuming more is always safer.** Never finish heavier than you started.
- **Buying sports drinks for a short gym session.** Water usually does the job.
- **Trying to fix hydration during a session.** Start hydrated instead.
- **Blaming every cramp on electrolytes.** That can delay finding the real cause.

## Key takeaways
- Dehydration of 2% of body mass or more is linked with poorer aerobic performance.
- For shorter, cooler or lighter sessions, drinking to thirst may be enough.
- Longer, hotter and harder sessions need planned drinking.
- Never gain weight during exercise. Suspected hyponatremia is an emergency.
- Sodium is the electrolyte lost most in sweat. The link between cramps and electrolytes is contested.
