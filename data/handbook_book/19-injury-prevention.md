## Chapter overview
Nearly every gym habit gets defended on injury grounds, and many of those defences are untested. This chapter starts with the finding that has real evidence behind it.

## The best-supported protection is training itself
> evidence: Evidence: strong
> Across six randomised trials with 7,738 people aged 12 to 40 and 177 injuries, strength training cut sports injury risk (relative risk 0.338, 0.238 to 0.480). Strength of evidence was rated high. A 10% rise in training volume cut injury risk by more than four percentage points. {lauersen2018}
> Limits: the review covered sports injuries in athletic groups. It shows regular strength training is protective in those groups. It does not tell you how often a given gym exercise hurts people.
So the most supported protection is the training itself, progressed sensibly and kept up for years. Anything that keeps you training consistently helps. Anything that keeps interrupting training has a cost, including too much caution.

## What common advice is worth
| Claim | Grade | Comment |
| Strength training lowers injury risk | Strong, in sports groups | More training was linked with more protection. {lauersen2018} |
| Warming up prevents injury | Not settled | Warm up because it improves your session. |
| Stretching prevents injury | Not settled | Do not rely on it for protection. |
| Progressing gradually lowers risk | Practical | Fits the evidence above. Not tested alone here. |
| Good technique lowers risk | Practical | Hard to test and widely accepted. |
| Machines are safer than free weights | Insufficient | They differ in joint demand and stability, which affects your choice. That is not a safety ranking. |

## Managing load
Most of what you control is here. It is practical guidance, not proven by a trial, but a sensible reason still counts for something.
- **Take the smallest step available.** The percentage jump is what matters.
- **Do not raise load and volume in the same week.** If something starts hurting, you will not know why.
- **Treat a return from a break as a return.** Do not restart at the loads you left at.
- **Be patient with tendons.** It is commonly explained that tendons adapt more slowly than muscle. No practical timeline is established, so treat this as a reason for patience, not a schedule.
- **Cut back when several exercises fall together.** See [[recovery|Recovery]] and [[deload-week|Deload week]].

## Technique and range
Good technique is widely accepted and hard to test directly. It stays a default because the reasoning is sound and following it costs nothing.
- **Load gained by shortening your range is not progress.** See [[leg-sessions|Building a leg session]] for when a partial is fair.
- **Stop a set when your technique changes.** This is more useful than hitting a repetition number, and it avoids the open question of how close to failure to go.

## Discomfort and pain
Hard training is uncomfortable. Discomfort and pain are different things, and it is worth learning the difference early.
| Expected | Stop and get it checked |
| Muscle fatigue and burning | Sharp, stabbing or localised joint pain |
| Breathing hard | Pain that worsens with each repetition |
| Soreness in the next days, especially after new work | Pain at rest, or pain that wakes you at night |
| A movement feeling heavy | Swelling, a joint giving way, or lost movement |
| Mild stiffness that eases as you warm up | Numbness, tingling or weakness in a specific pattern |
Anything in the right column means stop that exercise and find the cause. Training through it can turn a manageable problem into a long one.

## When to see a clinician
> warn: Beyond a training adjustment
> This Handbook does not diagnose. See a qualified clinician for:
> Pain that is sharp, localised, present at rest, or wakes you at night.
> Numbness, tingling or weakness in a specific pattern.
> Swelling, a joint giving way, or sudden loss of range.
> Any pain that has not improved after two weeks of truly reduced loading.
> Fatigue or falling performance that continues after several weeks of reduced training.
> Missed periods, frequent illness or bone pain alongside low food intake. These may point to low energy availability, a medical concern.
> Distress about food, weight or missed sessions that affects daily life.
> Getting checked early is not too cautious. It helps protect the consistency that matters most.

## A short session checklist
- Did I do warm-up sets on my first heavy exercise?
- Did those warm-up loads feel normal today?
- Did I keep full range on every working set?
- Did I stop each set when my technique changed?
- Did I change only one thing this week?
- Does anything match the right column above?

## Key takeaways
- The best-supported protection is strength training itself, progressed sensibly and kept up.
- Warm up for performance. Do not count on it to prevent injury.
- It is not settled that stretching prevents injury.
- Load management and technique are sensible defaults, not measured effects.
- Learn the difference between discomfort and pain. The right column means stop.
