## Chapter overview
Nutrition advice can make hundreds of decisions look equally important. They are not. A few things account for most of your result, and the rest only becomes worth your attention once those are handled.
This chapter sets out that order and the words the rest of this volume uses.

## The order of importance
This order is practical guidance, built on the sources cited below.
| Priority | What | Why it sits here |
| 1 | Total energy | Decides whether your weight goes up or down. Nothing below this overrides it. {aragon2017} |
| 2 | Total daily protein | Helps decide how much of a weight change is muscle. {issnprotein} |
| 3 | Sticking with it | A diet you abandon delivers nothing. Long-term success depends on it. {aragon2017} |
| 4 | Food quality, fibre, vitamins and minerals | Matters for health, fullness and how you feel while training. |
| 5 | Meal timing | Real but small once the rows above are in place. |
| 6 | Supplements | Last, for a reason. |
> tip: In practice
> Many people turn this list upside down, worrying about supplements and meal timing while their total food and protein go unmeasured. If rows 1 and 2 are not handled, nothing below them will make up for it.

## Energy
Calories are units of energy. Eating less than you use over time lowers your body mass. Eating more than you use raises it.
| Goal | Energy position |
| Fat loss | Eating below what you use, over time. |
| Staying the same | Eating about what you use. |
| Muscle gain | Eating a little above what you use, with enough protein and resistance training. |
> evidence: Evidence: strong
> The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) states that fat loss is driven by a sustained calorie deficit, and that a wide range of diets, from low-fat to low-carbohydrate and ketogenic, can be similarly effective for body composition. {aragon2017}
> The same paper says the apparent advantage of low-carbohydrate and ketogenic diets comes from their higher protein content. Intermittent fasting showed no significant advantage over daily calorie restriction. {aragon2017}
> Its authors note a shortage of research in women and older people. {aragon2017}
So use the eating pattern you can sustain. The pattern delivers the calories and protein. It does not do anything special by itself.

## The three macronutrients
| Nutrient | Energy | Main role here |
| Protein | About 4 kcal per gram | Supplies amino acids for repair and building muscle. |
| Carbohydrate | About 4 kcal per gram | A main fuel for repeated hard sets. Very low intake can show up as later sets fading. |
| Fat | About 9 kcal per gram | Needed for absorbing some vitamins and for normal health. You need enough, not as much as possible. |
A practical way to set your food: set protein first, because it has a clear target. Set fat next, at a level that keeps meals adequate and pleasant. Let carbohydrate fill the rest.
> warn: Carbohydrates are not the problem
> Carbohydrate restriction has no shown advantage once calories and protein are matched. {aragon2017} This matters in India: rice, roti, millets and dals are the affordable base of most meals. Cutting them out can hurt your training and make your diet harder to keep up.
> The more useful question is how much total energy a meal gives you, and whether you are meeting your protein target.

## Protein in brief
[[protein|Protein]] covers this properly. In brief: 1.4 to 2.0 g per kg of body weight a day is sufficient for most exercising people, and your daily total comes first. {issnprotein} Common Indian sources include milk, curd, paneer, soya chunks, tofu, dals, rajma, chana, eggs where eaten, and whey where affordable.

## Fibre and food quality
The top three rows decide body composition. Row four decides how you feel on the way, and your health over decades. That is a different consideration, not a smaller one.
- **Fibre.** WHO recommends adults get at least 25 g a day of naturally occurring fibre from food. Vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains supply it. {whocarb2023}
- **Fruit and vegetables.** Eat a good range.
- **Highly processed foods.** They are not forbidden. They are often high in calories and less filling, which can make a deficit harder to sustain. That is a practical argument, not a moral one.
> warn: Plant-based diets: nutrients that need attention
> A well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can support training. Some nutrients need attention, especially on a fully plant-based diet:
> **Vitamin B12.** Not reliably available from plant foods. Vegans need fortified foods or a supplement.
> **Iron.** Iron from plants is less well absorbed. A vitamin C source with the meal helps, and tea or coffee with the meal can reduce absorption.
> **Zinc.** Absorption is lower from diets high in phytate, found in whole grains and pulses.
> **Omega-3.** The body converts only a limited amount from plant sources.
> **Vitamin D.** For most people this depends more on sun exposure than on food.
> If you suspect a deficiency, get a blood test and have a clinician interpret it. Do not guess and self-prescribe.

## Water
Drink across the day, and expect to need more in hot weather or when you sweat heavily. [[hydration|Hydration and electrolytes]] covers this properly.

## Consistency over perfection
One meal does not undo your progress, and one meal does not create it. Your body responds to what you do most weeks, over months. This does not mean intake does not matter. It means choosing the right timescale, and it argues against the all-or-nothing pattern that ends many diets.

## When nutrition becomes a medical question
> warn: Get qualified support
> This Handbook does not diagnose, prescribe clinical diets or interpret medical results. See a qualified clinician or dietitian if any of these apply:
> A condition affected by diet, such as diabetes, kidney or liver disease, coeliac disease, thyroid disorders, PCOS or heart disease.
> Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
> A suspected deficiency, or abnormal blood results.
> Unintended weight loss, missed periods, frequent illness or bone pain. These may point to low energy availability, a medical concern.
> Distress about food, eating or body weight, restriction that feels hard to control, or eating that affects your daily life. Asking early is easier than asking late.

## Key takeaways
- Total energy, total protein and sticking with it account for most of the outcome.
- A wide range of diets can work. Choose the one you can sustain.
- Carbohydrate restriction has no shown advantage once calories and protein are matched.
- Set protein first, then enough fat, then carbohydrate with the rest.
- Plant-based diets can work. B12, iron, zinc, omega-3 and vitamin D need attention.
- Consistency over months beats precision on any one day.
