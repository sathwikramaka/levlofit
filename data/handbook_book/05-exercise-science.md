## Chapter overview
Training works because a signal, repeated often enough and recovered from well enough, changes your muscle. Exercise choice, sets, rest, food and sleep all exist to protect or strengthen that signal.
This chapter explains the signal and the few variables that control it. Read it once, then come back whenever a later recommendation seems arbitrary.

## What adaptation means
Adaptation is a change in your body that makes a hard demand easier. Lift a load your body is not yet equipped for, recover, and you become a little better equipped. Stop the demand and the change slowly reverses. That is also why a plan you have outgrown stops working even though nothing about it changed.
| Adaptation | What changes | Roughly how fast |
| Nervous system | Better coordination and confidence under load. The muscle is not bigger yet. | Days to weeks |
| Muscle | Muscle fibres grow thicker. | Weeks to months |
| Connective tissue | Tendons and ligaments slowly adapt. | Months |
These timescales are a teaching guide, not measured limits for you. The order still matters. Early strength gains come quickly and often before your body visibly changes, so do not expect the mirror to keep up with the bar in the first weeks.

## Tension: the main signal
The leading explanation for muscle growth is **mechanical tension**: the force a muscle fibre experiences as it works against resistance. Fibres that are recruited and work hard receive the signal to build.
The practical point is this: judge a set by whether its last repetitions were truly hard with acceptable technique. A set stopped far short of that is likely to do less.
> tip: In practice
> "Feeling the muscle" and "getting a pump" are sensations. They do not tell you by themselves that a set will build muscle.

## How muscle growth adds up
A common story says training damages muscle and the body repairs it bigger. The measurements do not support it as the main cause.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate
> In ten young men followed through ten weeks of training, muscle damage and protein synthesis were both highest in the first week, but neither was related to muscle growth. By weeks three and ten, damage had fallen and the rise in protein synthesis correlated strongly with growth (r about 0.9). This is one small study of mechanisms. {damas2016}
The authors conclude that growth comes from many small rises in protein synthesis, as damage fades. Two things follow in practice:
- **Soreness is not a score.** A new exercise or a long break can leave you very sore. A well-run session months later can leave you less sore and still be useful.
- **Do not chase damage.** Seeking soreness adds recovery cost, and that cost can come out of your next session.

## The dose
Once tension is the signal, planning comes down to three questions: how much hard work, how often, and how hard.
### Volume
Volume means hard working sets per muscle per week. It has the clearest link to growth.
> evidence: Evidence: strong for more volume, moderate for the shape of the curve
> The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand found that growth was enhanced by 10 or more sets per muscle group per week, with a dose-response. {acsm2026}
> A review of 67 studies found that size and strength both rise with volume, with diminishing returns that are much stronger for strength. Counting an assisting set as half a set predicted results best. {pelland2025}
So more tends to give more, and each extra set buys less. The Handbook gives no personal target above 10 sets, because your recovery, history and stress decide what you can use. Count a set that trains a muscle directly as one, and a set where it assists as half. Rows train your biceps. Presses train your triceps.
### Frequency
With weekly volume equal, training a muscle more often did not change growth. It does appear to help strength: the ACSM found strength was enhanced by 2 or more sessions a week, and could not determine an upper limit. {acsm2026} {pelland2025}
In practice, frequency is mainly a way to deliver volume. Spreading many sets across more days can keep the later sets from fading.
### How close to failure
**Repetitions in reserve (RIR)** is how many more repetitions you could have done with acceptable technique when you stopped. Two left is 2 RIR. No more possible is 0 RIR, called momentary failure.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate that hard training is enough; insufficient that failure adds anything
> A review of 15 studies found no advantage for growth from training to momentary failure over stopping short (effect size 0.12, confidence interval -0.13 to 0.37). A looser definition of failure gave a trivial advantage (0.19, 0.00 to 0.37). The authors suggest a possible non-linear relationship. {refalo2023}
> A later analysis found growth improved as sets ended closer to failure, while strength barely changed. Its authors call it exploratory, because the RIR values were estimated. {robinson2024}
> The ACSM reports that training to momentary fatigue did not consistently affect outcomes, and its main recommendation is to train with high effort. {acsm2026}
These fit together if benefit grows as a set becomes truly hard, then flattens before failure. That is a hypothesis, not a proven curve.
| Goal | Reasonable default | Why |
| Muscle | About 1 to 3 repetitions left | A practical default. Growth may suffer if you stop far short, and failure has not been shown to add more. |
| Strength | 2 or more left | Strength looks similar across a wide range. |
| Both | Failure not required | Not shown to be necessary for either. |

## Rest between sets
Shorter rest makes training feel harder. That does not make it more productive.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate for a small benefit past about a minute; insufficient that short rest harms growth
> A review of 9 studies found intervals crossing zero at every site: arm 0.13 (-0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (-0.13 to 0.43), whole body -0.08 (-0.45 to 0.29). The authors read this as a small benefit from resting more than 60 seconds, and no clear further gain past 90 seconds. Six of the studies used untrained people, and they ran for only 5 to 10 weeks. {schoenfeld2024}
> The ACSM found no strength difference between rest under and over one minute, and not enough data to say whether rest length affects growth. {acsm2026}
> An earlier review concluded that the research does not support needing shorter rest for growth than for strength. {henselmans2014}
Rest until the next set is limited by the target muscle, not by your breathing. Heavy compound lifts usually need longer to perform well. Cutting rest is a fair way to fit a session into less time. Treat it as a time choice, not as progress.

## Fatigue and readiness
A hard session leaves a signal and leaves fatigue. How you feel in the next day or two mostly reflects the fatigue, so that is a poor time to judge your plan.
Older books draw a neat "supercompensation" curve with a perfect window for your next session. Train, recover and come back stronger is sound. A window you can time is **not established**. Use what you can observe instead: session performance, joint comfort, sleep, appetite and motivation. [[recovery|Recovery]] covers these.

## Specificity
Your body adapts to what you actually do. Heavy, low-repetition work builds the skill of lifting heavy. Plenty of hard work in a moderate range builds more size. These overlap but are not identical.
It also applies to movements. You mostly get better at the exercise you practise, so keep exercises long enough to progress on them.

## Individual differences
People respond differently to the same training. Genes play a part. So do sleep, food, stress, history and skill, and those you can influence. Averages tell you where to start, not where you will end up. Your own log, over months, becomes the better guide.

## Limits of this chapter
Most of this comes from studies lasting weeks to months, mostly in young adults, often men. The direction is fairly consistent. The precision is not, which is why you will see ranges rather than exact numbers.
> warn: See a clinician
> This chapter describes normal training in healthy adults. Persistent pain, unexplained loss of strength, or symptoms that do not settle with rest are not things to adjust in your training. See a qualified clinician.

## Key takeaways
- Take working sets close enough that the last repetitions are hard. Failure is not required.
- Muscle damage is not what drives growth. Soreness is a poor measure of progress.
- Growth rises with volume, with diminishing returns. Count assisting sets as half.
- With volume equal, frequency does not change growth, but it does help strength.
- Rest long enough for the target muscle to limit the next set. Cutting rest is a time choice.
- Early strength gains arrive first. Judge a plan over months.
