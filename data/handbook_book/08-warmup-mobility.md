## Chapter overview
A warm-up has one well-supported benefit: a better session. Other benefits are claimed on much weaker grounds. This chapter separates them and gives you a warm-up short enough that you will keep doing it.
Mobility is here because the two get confused. A warm-up prepares you for today. Mobility work changes your range over months.

## What a warm-up does
The aim is to get ready, not tired.
| What it does | How |
| Raises muscle temperature | Light continuous activity. |
| Rehearses the movement | Practising the exact lift with gradually heavier loads. |
| Tests the day | Rising loads show how today is going before a hard set. |
> evidence: Evidence: emerging
> A review of passive heating, such as baths, saunas and heated clothing, found warmer muscle develops force faster. Maximal voluntary force did not rise. A warmer muscle gets to a force sooner. It does not become stronger. This review did not study active warm-ups, which do much more than raise temperature. {rodrigues2022}

## Claimed benefits, and their grades
| Claimed benefit | Grade | What is supported |
| Better performance today | Moderate | Dynamic stretching improves jumping and sprinting compared with no warm-up. This is the benefit to rely on. {li2023} |
| Faster force | Emerging | Warmer muscle produces force faster, without raising maximal force. {rodrigues2022} |
| More range today | Moderate | A single session of static stretching reduces stiffness. {ingram2025} |
| Feeling ready | Practical | A real benefit that research has not settled. Use it if it helps. |
| Less injury | Not settled | Do not treat this as your reason to warm up. See below. |
> warn: Injury risk
> "Warm up so you do not get injured" is the most repeated reason, and not a secure one. Do not count on a warm-up or stretching to protect you.
> The stronger evidence points to strength training itself. Across six randomised trials with 7,738 people aged 12 to 40, it cut sports injury risk by about two thirds (relative risk 0.338, 0.238 to 0.480), and more training was linked with more protection. Most of those people were athletes, so applying this to gym training is a reasonable step, not a tested one. {lauersen2018}

## Dynamic and static stretching
**Dynamic stretching** moves a joint through its range under control, such as leg swings, arm circles, bodyweight squats and walking lunges. **Static stretching** holds a stretched position still.
| Approach | Effect on jumping and sprinting |
| Dynamic alone | Improved jump height and sprint time. The steady choice. |
| Static, then dynamic | Ranked highest for jump height, and clearly better than no warm-up. |
| Static alone, just before | Ranked lowest for jump height, and made sprint time worse. |
| Foam rolling | No significant difference from no warm-up. |
> evidence: Evidence: moderate
> This comes from a network analysis of 35 studies, with dynamic stretching for 7 to 10 minutes showing the largest effect. Most studies were not randomised and few used blinding, so treat the ranking as a guide. {li2023}
> Hold length matters too. Static stretching of 60 seconds or more per muscle cut performance by 4.6%, against 1.1% for under 60 seconds. {behm2016}
In practice:
- If a stretch lets you reach a position you otherwise cannot, use it, keep it short, then do dynamic movement and warm-up sets.
- If you have no such limit, dynamic work alone is enough and faster.
- Avoid long static holds as your only preparation right before heavy or explosive work.
These studies measured jumping and sprinting, which may react more than an ordinary set of 8 to 12 repetitions.

## A warm-up in four stages
| Stage | Time | Purpose |
| General | 3 to 5 min | Light continuous activity to raise temperature. |
| Dynamic mobility | 4 to 6 min | Controlled movement through the ranges you will use today. |
| Activation | 0 to 5 min | Optional light work for muscles you struggle to feel. |
| Warm-up sets | 5 to 10 min | Your first big lift, with gradually heavier loads. |
These times are practical guides. **Warm-up sets matter most**, and they are the stage people skip. They rehearse the exact movement, and they tell you something: when a normal load feels heavy today, you find out before committing to a hard set. If you are short of time, cut the general stage, not these.
> tip: On activation drills
> Glute bridges before squats and band work before pressing are popular and cheap. It is **not established** that they add anything beyond warm-up sets. Keep them if they help you. Drop them before you drop your warm-up sets.
Later exercises in a session need only one or two light sets.

## Mobility
Mobility is the range you can actively reach and control. Range you can only be pushed into is not much use under load.
> evidence: Evidence: moderate
> A review of 65 studies found stretching does not lengthen muscle: fascicle length did not change after one session or many. Stiffness fell after both. Tolerance to the stretch rose only after repeated stretching. {ingram2025}
> Regular stretching builds range, and in one review its volume, intensity and frequency did not play a significant role in how much. {konrad2024}
> Strength training through a full range gave range of motion gains similar to stretching. {afonso2021}
What this means for you:
- **Train mobility separately.** A warm-up uses the range you have. New range takes weeks to months.
- **Pick a method you will keep up.** Regular practice matters. Exact hold times matter less than people claim.
- **Lift through your full range.** It builds usable range while you train anyway.
- **Target the real limit.** A muscle can feel tight without being what limits you. Test one position, work on it, and retest weeks later.
> warn: Stretching into pain
> Mild discomfort is expected. Sharp, radiating or joint-line pain is not. Stop, and see a qualified clinician rather than stretching harder.

## Warm up for today's session
| Session | Focus | Typical movements |
| Push | Shoulders, chest, triceps, upper back | Arm circles, band pull-aparts, upper back rotations, push-up variations |
| Pull | Lats, upper back, rear shoulders | Shoulder blade drills, dead hangs, light rows |
| Legs | Hips, knees, ankles, glutes | Bodyweight squats, walking lunges, leg swings, ankle rocks |

## Common mistakes
- **Skipping warm-up sets to save time.** Your first hard set becomes the warm-up.
- **Long static holds as your only preparation.** Follow them with dynamic work. {li2023}
- **Warming up until tired.** Getting breathless uses energy your working sets need.
- **Treating foam rolling as a performance boost.** Use it if it makes a position more comfortable.
- **Expecting a warm-up to fix a mobility limit.** That takes months.
- **Relying on the warm-up for injury protection.** The evidence points to strength training. {lauersen2018}

## Key takeaways
- The demonstrated benefit of warming up is a better session.
- Warmer muscle produces force faster, not more maximal force.
- Dynamic work helps. Long static holds alone just before explosive work do not.
- Warm-up sets matter most and double as a recovery check. Activation drills are optional.
- Stretching does not lengthen muscle. Range improves through lower stiffness and more tolerance.
- Protection from injury is better supported for strength training than for warming up.
